Embedding-Based Candidate Retrieval over Japan's Corporate Registry

Bachelor's thesis, Tokyo International University, 2026

Python, EmbeddingGemma, sentence-transformers, FAISS (HNSW)

The question

Finding links between companies is central to due diligence and anti-money-laundering work. But Japan's public corporate registry has no ownership data, and the representative field is filled for only 1.3% of Tokyo companies. That leaves two fields that every record has: the company name and the registered address.

I asked whether those two fields are enough to surface real business associates, and how much of what they surface is only a surface resemblance.

What I did

  1. Took all 1,173,931 active Tokyo companies from the government's gBizINFO dataset.
  2. Normalized each name and address (Unicode, dashes, whitespace) and joined them into one text "fingerprint" per company.
  3. Embedded every fingerprint with EmbeddingGemma, a 300M-parameter multilingual model.
  4. Searched for each company's 10 nearest neighbours with FAISS, replacing 9.3 × 1011 possible comparisons with fast approximate search, and kept pairs above 0.90 cosine similarity.
  5. Merged the 130,572 resulting pairs into 39,563 clusters and reviewed them by hand.

What I found

  • It finds relationships written into the registration. Duplicate records, serially named special-purpose vehicles (like 東京レジ1 to 39) and paired securitisation structures come out with high confidence. All eight of the largest clusters, holding 3,043 companies, were families of this kind.
  • It misses ordinary corporate groups whose links aren't visible in their names or addresses.
  • It fails where shared words and shared buildings chain unrelated companies together. One cluster was hundreds of unrelated firms that happened to use the same English word in their names.
  • The usable threshold is narrow. At 0.85, a quarter of all companies collapse into one giant cluster. At 0.95, the largest real structures break apart.

Why it matters

The method works best on exactly the kind of entity that beneficial-ownership research is most concerned about: single-purpose vehicles registered in batches and administered side by side. It isn't a relationship detector, but it can turn a registry of over a million companies into a short, reviewable list for compliance teams. The study also measures how far Japan's open registry data can go for network analysis.
